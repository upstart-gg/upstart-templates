The plates are pushed back and somebody says, tell them about the time with the canoe. And you do. You have told it twenty times. You know where to slow down, which uncle to leave out, and how long to wait before the last line.

That is an essay. It has been drafted in front of readers for ten years, and they have been giving you notes the whole time, with their faces.

## What goes wrong on the page

On the page, the same person starts with the weather. They explain who everyone is. They put the canoe on page four. When I ask why, they say that it seemed too simple just to tell it.

So in week one, I ask for exactly that. Tell it on the page the way you tell it at dinner. Five hundred words, no fixing. Then we read it aloud and listen for the place where your voice changes, the half-second where you stop performing.

## The part you skip

There is nearly always a part you skip at dinner. It is too long, or your sister is at the table. Often the essay is in there, behind the canoe. The story you keep telling is how you have been keeping it company.

You don’t need a new idea. You need to stay at the table a little longer than is polite.
